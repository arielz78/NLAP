---

Recap a piece of work from this session — but use active recall (retrieval
practice / scaffolded retrieval), not passive review. This is a learning tool,
not just a summary. Follow the three rounds **in order**, do not collapse or
skip any of them — the whole point is to keep Ariel actively retrieving for as
long as possible before switching to passive review.

1. **Identify the target.** If Ariel named a topic, use that. Otherwise default
   to the most substantial piece of reasoning/work from this session (the thing
   with the most decision points or the most "we changed direction here" moments
   — not just the most recent message).

2. **Round 1 — open quiz. Then STOP.** Ask Ariel to attempt the recall cold,
   with no scaffolding:
   > "Before I lay it out — walk me through what you remember: what happened,
   > in what order, and *why* each turn happened the way it did. Don't worry
   > about getting it perfect, the gaps are the useful part."

   Wait for their response. Do not give any part of the answer in this turn —
   that defeats the purpose (no retrieval gap, no encoding benefit).

3. **Round 2 — targeted hints. Then STOP again.** Silently map what Ariel got
   right vs. what's missing or off (don't show this assessment yet — that's
   round 3's job). For each gap, write a **hint that points at the gap's
   location without naming its content** — aim at the spot, not the answer.
   Examples of the right altitude:
   - "There's a turn early on where I gave you a confidence-based answer and
     you pushed back on it — what was that about, and why did it matter?"
   - "You named the upgrades, but what's the *pattern* connecting which sources
     got upgraded and which didn't — it's not about complexity."
   - "There's a whole second phase after the upgrades were found — a different
     *kind* of check entirely. What was being verified there, and why?"

   Present these as a numbered list. Tell Ariel explicitly this is round 2 —
   take another pass armed with these. Then **stop and wait** for their second
   attempt. Do not reveal answers inside the hints themselves.

4. **Round 3 — the full long-form recap.** This is *not* a condensed comparison
   summary — it's the complete, in-depth narrative version: the full logic
   chain, told turn by turn, with the reasoning and the "why" behind each pivot
   spelled out in real depth (not just "what happened" but *why it happened
   that way, what the alternative read would have been, and why that turn
   mattered*). Ariel wants the longer version that actually reconstructs the
   thinking — texture and depth, not a bulleted highlight reel.

   Weave the comparison into this narrative rather than bolting it on at the
   end:
   - As you narrate each turn, note in-line where Ariel's round 1 or round 2
     attempts already landed on it (reinforcement matters as much as
     correction — name it explicitly when a hint successfully triggered the
     right retrieval; that's a signal worth reflecting back)
   - Where something still didn't surface across both attempts, narrate that
     piece in full and be precise about *where* the gap was (a missing step, a
     reversed order, a "why" that didn't land). Don't soften this — per
     Ariel's standing preference the honest read beats the comfortable one —
     but frame it as "here's the piece that didn't stick" rather than "wrong,"
     since the goal is encoding, not grading
   - End with **the transferable principle** — the one-sentence "what's the
     lesson here that applies beyond this specific case." This is the piece
     most likely to actually stick and transfer forward to the next audit or
     decision.

5. Keep the round-3 recap self-contained — Ariel shouldn't need to scroll back
   through the conversation to cross-reference it. The full narrative,
   reasoning, comparisons, and principle all belong in one place, delivered
   once, in depth.
